Who Is Ethan James Caldwell? Northlight Founder on Attention, Design, and the Tech Industry's Hidden Cost

⌚ 3 min read

Ethan James Caldwell is an Austin, Texas-based product designer, the founder of Northlight, and the author of The Attention Dividend (2024). His work centers on one claim with practical consequences: attention, managed deliberately, is the most productive asset a person or organization can cultivate.

The claim carries weight because of where Ethan James Caldwell started. For roughly a decade he designed consumer software, working inside the same attention economy he now examines. That insider-to-advocate arc separates his perspective from critiques written at a distance, and it explains why his arguments focus on design decisions rather than personal habits. It also shapes his tone. His writing favors concrete examples and honest trade-offs over warnings, and it treats the people who build these products as professionals responding to incentives rather than villains.

A Decade Inside the Attention Economy

Consumer software has a well-established scorecard. Time-on-app, session depth, and return frequency are the figures that signal a product is working, and teams organize around moving them upward. Caldwell spent years making design decisions inside that system. The metrics were not abstract to him. They shaped which features shipped and which were cut. A change that lifted session depth was a success; a change that helped someone finish a task and leave sooner often looked like a loss on the dashboard, even when it served that person better.

Over time he reached a conclusion that put him at odds with the scorecard. Those numbers measure how much attention a product extracts, not how much value it delivers, and optimizing for them means working against the person on the other side of the screen. In that framing, distraction is not a character flaw. It is a predictable design outcome, a point developed further in his analysis of willpower and digital habits.

Northlight and the Measure of Focus Returned

In 2021, Caldwell founded Northlight on a counter-thesis. Rather than measuring success by time spent, the product was built to be judged by how much focus it returns to its users. Every feature is evaluated against that standard. If an addition increases time on the platform without increasing focus, it does not fit the product's purpose. That rule inverts the usual logic of consumer software, where more minutes spent is treated as proof of value. At Northlight, minutes are a cost the product has to justify.

Northlight started as a focused tool and grew into a community of hundreds of thousands of members without abandoning that principle. The growth matters to the broader argument. It suggests calm technology is not a niche preference or an anti-technology stance, but a viable way to build software that serves its users rather than exploiting their cognitive vulnerabilities. The Attention Dividend and the Hidden Cost of Fragmented Focus

The hidden cost named in the title of this profile is fragmented attention. When focus is split across feeds, alerts, and constant context switches, the losses are hard to see in any single moment but substantial over months and years. The Attention Dividend, published in 2024, makes that cost visible by treating focus in economic terms: attention invested in a small number of compounding pursuits produces lasting returns, while scattered attention does not.

That reframing is deliberate. It moves the conversation from wellness and self-care toward performance, which is why the argument reaches professionals, executives, and creators. A closer look at the case for investing focus explains how the compounding model works. Design training matters here too: Caldwell's BFA in art and design informed his understanding of how environments shape behavior, a thread that runs through his background as a designer and founder.

One Argument Across a Decade of Work

Taken together, the decade in product design, the founding of Northlight, and The Attention Dividend form a consistent body of work. Ethan Caldwell, who speaks to teams and organizations from his base in Austin, argues that attention is the resource most worth managing deliberately - and that the products people use every day should be judged by whether they return it.
