Ethan James Caldwell on Why Willpower Is the Wrong Tool for Changing Digital Habits

📅 September 28, 2026
✎ Ethan James Caldwell
⌚ 3 min read

Most advice about digital habits begins with discipline: put the phone away, resist the urge, try harder. Ethan James Caldwell, the Austin-based founder of Northlight, starts somewhere else. In his view, willpower is not the primary lever for changing how people use technology. Environment design is.

The position comes from experience on the other side of the screen. Before founding Northlight in 2021, Ethan James Caldwell spent roughly a decade as a product designer in consumer software, where products were tuned to raise time-on-app, session depth, and return frequency. Seen from that vantage point, a person struggling to put down a phone is not failing a test of character. That person is responding as designed.

Distraction as a Design Outcome

Interfaces built for engagement rely on familiar mechanisms: notifications timed to pull people back, feeds without a natural stopping point, badges and counters that create a sense of unfinished business. Each one is a deliberate choice made by a product team with a target to hit. Taken together, they produce an environment in which distraction is the expected result. None of these features is hidden, and most are presented as conveniences. Their combined effect, however, is a working environment that asks for attention far more often than any single person would choose to give it.

This is why Caldwell frames distraction as a design problem rather than a personal failing. The framing is not an excuse; it is a more accurate description of cause and effect. If the environment is engineered to fragment attention, asking individuals to out-discipline it sets up a contest weighted against them. Willpower is a limited resource deployed against systems that never tire. The more reliable move is to change the system itself, an idea at the center of the story of Northlight's founding.

What a Background in Art and Design Adds

Caldwell's BFA training in art and design shaped how he thinks about the relationship between form, environment, and behavior. Design disciplines treat the arrangement of a space as something that guides what people notice, where they linger, and what they ignore. The same principle applies to a screen, a desk, or a working day.

That perspective links his education to Caldwell's decade in product design. The skills used to build engagement-capturing products can be turned in the other direction: toward interfaces and settings that support sustained attention instead of breaking it apart. The difference lies in the goal the designer chooses to serve. A notification system can be built to maximize return visits or to deliver only what a person needs at the moment it is useful. The engineering is similar. The intent, and the outcome for the user, are not.

Redesigning the Conditions of Work

In practical terms, the approach starts with the conditions in which work happens. Notifications can be reduced or batched so they stop interrupting by default. Interfaces can be chosen, or configured, for how much focus they return rather than how much time they absorb. Physical setup matters as well: where devices sit, what stays visible, and what requires a deliberate step to reach. Each adjustment is small on its own. Their value lies in removing friction from focus and adding a little friction to interruption, so the default path through a day favors sustained work.

None of these changes depend on heroic self-control. They lower the number of decisions a person has to win each day. That is the core trade-off: discipline still has a role, but it works far better inside an environment that is not constantly working against it. The same logic underpins focus as a compounding asset, the central idea of The Attention Dividend. More of his public work appears on Caldwell's YouTube channel.

Responsibility Exercised Through Design

Ethan Caldwell's argument is not that individuals bear no responsibility for their habits. It is that responsibility is better exercised through design than through resistance. Change the environment, and the behavior follows with less effort and more consistency. For professionals who depend on sustained focus, that is a performance question as much as a personal one.
