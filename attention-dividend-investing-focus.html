The Attention Dividend: Ethan James Caldwell's Case for Investing Focus, Not Just Protecting It

📅 October 6, 2026
✎ Ethan James Caldwell
⌚ 3 min read

Ethan James Caldwell's 2024 book, The Attention Dividend, starts from a premise that sets it apart from most writing on digital wellbeing: focus is not only something to protect. It is something to invest. The Austin, Texas-based author and Northlight founder treats attention as an economic asset, one that compounds when it is directed deliberately.

The framing is intentional. Ethan James Caldwell spent roughly a decade as a product designer in consumer software before founding Northlight in 2021, and that background gives the book its grounding. It is written from the perspective of someone who helped build the systems that compete for attention, and who later built a product designed to return it.

From Protecting Focus to Investing It

Much of the conversation around technology and attention is defensive. It centers on blocking, limiting, and recovering, all of which treat focus as a resource under threat. The Attention Dividend accepts that the threat exists but argues that defense is only half the picture. Protected attention that is never put to deliberate use produces little. Turning off notifications creates room, but it does not decide what fills that room. Without a clear direction, reclaimed time tends to drift back toward whatever is most immediately available.

The book's alternative is investment. Attention directed into a small number of pursuits builds on itself: skills deepen, work gains continuity, and each period of sustained focus makes the next one more productive. Scattered attention does not compound in the same way. It is spent in fragments, and the returns stay fragmentary. The comparison to financial investing is direct: concentrated, patient allocation tends to outperform activity spread thinly across many positions, and the cost of constant switching resembles a fee charged on every transaction.

Focus directed deliberately compounds. Scattered attention is simply spent.

Why the Economic Frame Matters

By describing focus in economic terms, Caldwell moves digital wellbeing out of the self-care category and into a conversation about performance and productivity. That shift changes the audience. Professionals, executives, and creators who might dismiss wellness advice have a direct interest in an argument about how their most productive asset is allocated. The question stops being whether a habit feels healthy and becomes whether it produces work of lasting value.

It also changes how organizations can think about the tools they adopt. If attention is an asset, then software that fragments it carries a cost, even when that cost never appears on a balance sheet. Calm, in this view, becomes a competitive advantage: teams able to sustain depth can do work that reactive, constantly interrupted teams struggle to match. Urgency is easy to measure and easy to reward. Depth is slower to show results, which is why the advantage of calm is underappreciated even by organizations that would benefit most from it.

Northlight as a Working Model

The book's argument has a practical counterpart in Northlight. Since its founding in 2021, the product has been evaluated by one measure: whether a feature increases focus, not whether it increases time on the platform. Northlight grew from a focused tool into a community of hundreds of thousands of members while holding to that standard, an outcome that tests the book's thesis beyond the page.

The method behind it ties back to design. Caldwell's view that environment design beats willpower explains how the investment is made in practice: by shaping notifications, interfaces, and physical setup so that sustained focus becomes the default. The same thread runs through Caldwell's career before Northlight. More on the book is available through his author website.

Treating Focus Like Capital

The Attention Dividend asks readers to treat focus the way a careful investor treats capital: allocate it deliberately, concentrate it where returns compound, and account for the cost of spreading it too thin. For Ethan Caldwell, that is less a lifestyle recommendation than an economic one, and it is the idea connecting his decade in product design, the founding of Northlight, and his work as an author in Austin.
